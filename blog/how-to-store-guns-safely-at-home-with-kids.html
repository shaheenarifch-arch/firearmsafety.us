Quick answer

To store guns safely at home with kids, keep every firearm unloaded and locked in a safe, lockbox or with a locking device, store the ammunition locked in a separate place, and keep the keys and codes where children cannot reach them. The American Academy of Pediatrics says the safest home for a child is one without guns, and that if a home does have them, they should be stored unloaded and locked with the ammunition locked away separately. Hiding a gun is not storage: children are curious, they search, and they often know where things are kept.

  • Unloaded: every firearm, every time it goes into storage.
  • Locked: in a safe or lockbox, or with a cable or trigger lock as a minimum.
  • Separate: ammunition locked in a different container or room.
  • Controlled: keys and codes kept on an adult, never in a drawer a child can open.

Key takeaways

  • Locked, unloaded storage with ammunition kept separately is the standard pediatricians and safety groups recommend for any home where children live or visit.
  • Firearm injuries became the leading cause of death for US children and teens aged 1 to 19 in 2020, according to a University of Michigan analysis of CDC data.
  • A safe or lockbox is stronger than a cable or trigger lock, but a lock that is actually used beats a safe that is left open.
  • Many local police departments hand out free cable locks through Project ChildSafe, so cost does not have to be a barrier.
  • Storage rules and child access laws differ by state. California, for example, has its own requirements under Penal Code 25100 and SB 53.
  • Teaching children what to do if they find a gun helps, but it is a backup to locked storage, never a replacement for it.

Why locked storage matters more than hiding

Most parents who keep a firearm at home are careful people. The problem is that careful hiding and safe storage are not the same thing. A gun on a closet shelf, in a nightstand or under a mattress is only as safe as a child's lack of curiosity, and children are built to explore.

Research over the past two decades has shown how often parents underestimate what their children know. A widely cited 2006 study of families with guns at home found that many children knew where the household gun was kept, and some reported having handled it, even when their parents believed they had not. Children watch where adults go, hear where things are put and find spare keys in the same drawers adults use.

The stakes are high. A University of Michigan team reviewing CDC mortality data reported in the New England Journal of Medicine that firearm injuries overtook motor vehicle crashes in 2020 as the leading cause of death for Americans aged 1 to 19. That number covers many causes, including unintentional shootings, suicide and assault. Locked storage cannot solve all of them, but it directly reduces the chance that a child or teen gets unsupervised access to a gun in their own home.

The good news is that safe storage is simple, inexpensive and fully within a parent's control. You do not need to change your views on firearms to make a gun much harder for a child to reach. You need a lock, a separate place for ammunition and a routine you follow every time.

The core rule: unloaded, locked, ammunition separate

Every safe storage recommendation for families comes back to three layers. Each one adds time and difficulty between a child and a working firearm, and together they make an accident far less likely.

1. Store it unloaded

Before a firearm goes into storage, unload it completely and confirm it is empty by checking both visually and physically, following the manual for your specific model. If you are not sure how to do this for your firearm, a beginner safety class or the manufacturer's instructions are the right place to learn. An unloaded gun in storage removes the single most dangerous condition.

2. Lock the firearm itself

The firearm should be locked inside a container, such as a gun safe or lockbox, or fitted with a locking device such as a cable lock. A container is generally the stronger choice because it protects the whole firearm and is harder to defeat. A cable lock is a reasonable minimum, especially while you shop for a safe.

3. Lock the ammunition separately

Ammunition should be stored locked and in a different place from the firearm. Splitting them means a child who gets past one lock still does not have both parts. A small locking box or a locked drawer in another room works well for most households.

Think of these as layers rather than choices. One layer is better than none, two is better than one, and all three is the standard to aim for in any home with children.

How to set up safe storage in one afternoon

You can move most homes from unsafe to safe storage in a few hours. Work through these steps with no children in the room.

  1. Find every firearm in the home. Include guns kept by other adults, inherited guns in boxes and anything stored in a car or garage. Ask each adult in the household directly.
  2. Unload each one and confirm it is empty. Follow the manual for each model. If you are unsure, pause and get help from a certified instructor rather than guessing.
  3. Pick the storage for each firearm. Use the comparison above. Note what you have now and what you plan to add.
  4. Install and anchor. Bolt safes and lockboxes to the floor or a wall stud where the instructions allow, or cable them to something fixed, so they cannot simply be carried away.
  5. Lock the ammunition separately. Choose a different room or at least a different locked container.
  6. Decide where keys and codes live. Keys go on an adult's key ring or in a place children truly cannot reach. Codes are never a birthday, an address or anything a child could guess.
  7. Change default codes. Many safes ship with a factory code. Reset it using the manual before you store anything inside.
  8. Test it. Close and lock everything, then check from a child's point of view: is there a key in sight, a code written down or a container that opens without the key?
  9. Write down your routine. Agree as a household that every firearm goes back into storage unloaded and locked immediately after use, with no exceptions for "just a minute."

Keys, codes and backup keys: the weak spot most families miss

A safe is only as secure as the way it is opened. Many storage failures happen not because a child broke a lock, but because they found the key or watched the code being typed.

The photo at the top of this guide shows a common mistake: an electronic safe with its backup key left sitting in the lock. Most keypad safes come with an override key in case the batteries die. That key needs the same care as the code. Store it away from the safe, never in the lock, never taped underneath and never in an obvious drawer nearby.

  • Shield the keypad: enter codes with your body between the keypad and anyone in the room, including older children.
  • Change codes if you suspect they are known: after a babysitter, a guest or a curious teen has been around, a new code costs nothing.
  • Replace batteries on a schedule: a dead battery tempts people to leave the safe open. Many households pick a date, such as daylight saving changes, to swap them.
  • Be careful with biometric safes: fingerprint readers can be convenient, but check the enrollment settings so only the intended adults are registered.
  • Count your keys: know how many keys each lock came with, and where every one of them is.

Storage by age: toddlers, school-age children and teens

Locked storage is the rule at every age, but the risks shift as children grow, and so does what they understand.

Toddlers and preschoolers

Young children cannot reliably understand danger or follow safety rules, even if they can repeat them. They climb, open drawers and put things in their mouths. For this age, the only protection is that a firearm and its ammunition are physically out of reach and locked. Remember that toddlers can push chairs to reach high shelves, so height alone is not a lock.

School-age children

Children from about five to twelve are curious and often have seen guns on screens, where they look like toys and the consequences are not real. This is the age to teach a clear rule about what to do if they find a gun, covered below. It is also the age when children start visiting friends' homes alone, so it is the time to start asking other parents about storage.

Teenagers

Teens can be capable and responsible, but they can also act on impulse, and some go through periods of intense stress. Locked storage with keys and codes they do not have protects them during those moments. If a teen is struggling with their mental health, many health organizations advise that firearms be stored outside the home temporarily, for example with a trusted relative or through a local storage option where available. If you or someone in your home is in crisis, you can call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the US.

If your teen is interested in firearms, a supervised beginner safety course taught by a certified instructor is a far better path than learning from videos or friends. It builds respect for the rules and gives you a shared vocabulary for talking about safety at home.

Visitors, playdates and other people's homes

Your storage only protects your home. Children spend time at grandparents' houses, friends' homes and with babysitters, and visitors sometimes bring firearms into your home.

Asking before a playdate

The AAP encourages parents to ask whether there is a gun in a home where their child will play, and how it is stored. It can feel awkward, but most parents appreciate the question once it is asked simply and without judgment. A friendly way to ask: "We ask everyone this before playdates. Are there any guns in the house, and are they locked up?" You can ask about pets, pools and allergies in the same conversation so it feels routine.

When guests bring a firearm

If a visiting relative or friend carries a firearm, it is reasonable to ask them to keep it locked in their vehicle in a secure container where that is legal, or to lock it in your safe while they visit. Bags and coats left on beds or chairs are easy for children to reach.

Grandparents and older relatives

Many grandparents kept firearms long before grandchildren arrived, and storage habits may not have changed. A respectful conversation before a visit, and an offer to help set up a lockbox, can protect children without making anyone feel accused.

What to teach children if they find a gun

Teaching is a backup layer, not a lock. Studies have found that even children who have been taught a rule may still handle a gun they find, especially younger ones. That is why storage comes first. Still, a simple, repeated rule gives children something to fall back on outside your home.

Many safety programs teach a version of these four steps:

  1. Stop. Do not move toward it.
  2. Do not touch it. Treat every gun as if it could be real and loaded, even if it looks like a toy.
  3. Leave the area. Walk away, and take friends with you if you can.
  4. Tell an adult right away. A parent, teacher or the adult in charge.

Practice it the way you practice crossing the street: short, calm and often. Praise a child who tells you they saw a gun, and never punish them for reporting it, or they may not tell you next time.

What the law says about storage (and why you should check your state)

Storage laws in the United States are a patchwork. This section is general information, not legal advice. Always confirm the current rules for your state with official sources such as your state attorney general or legislature.

  • Federal law: under 18 U.S.C. 922(z), licensed dealers generally must provide a secure gun storage or safety device with each handgun they sell. That is one reason many new handguns come with a cable lock in the box.
  • Child access prevention laws: many states have laws that can hold adults responsible when a child gets access to a firearm that was not stored safely. The details, including the age of the child and what counts as safe storage, differ from state to state.
  • California: California has some of the most detailed storage rules in the country, including Penal Code 25100 on child access and SB 53, which adds secure storage requirements at home. Our plain-English guide to the California safe storage law SB 53 walks through what counts as secure storage there.
  • Vehicles and travel: rules for storing a firearm in a car can be different from home rules and can change when you cross state lines. Check each state on your route.

Even where the law does not require locked storage, it is still the standard recommended by pediatricians and safety organizations for any home with children.

Common safe storage mistakes

  • Relying on a hiding place: a high shelf, a shoebox or a nightstand drawer is not a lock.
  • Leaving the key in or near the lock: including backup keys on keypad safes.
  • Storing ammunition with the gun: it removes one of your layers of protection.
  • Using a lock on a loaded firearm: trigger and cable locks are designed for unloaded guns. Follow the device's instructions.
  • "Just for a minute": many incidents happen when a firearm is set down briefly during cleaning, unpacking or a phone call.
  • Forgetting the car: a firearm left in a glove box or console can be reached by children in the back seat or taken in a break-in.
  • Assuming older kids are fine: teens still benefit from not having keys or codes.
  • Never revisiting the setup: as children grow taller and smarter, recheck what they can reach and what they may have figured out.

Free locks, training and other help

Cost and know-how should not stand between your family and safe storage.

  • Free cable locks: Project ChildSafe, a program of the National Shooting Sports Foundation, works with local law enforcement agencies to distribute free safety kits that include a cable lock. Call your local police department or sheriff's office and ask whether they take part.
  • Your pediatrician: many pediatricians are happy to talk about storage at a checkup, and some clinics hand out locks.
  • A beginner safety class: a certified instructor can show you how to unload and check your specific firearm, use your storage device correctly and talk through storage at home. See our guide to choosing a class.

Bottom line

If there is a firearm in a home with children, the safest setup is simple: unloaded, locked, with ammunition locked separately and keys and codes under adult control. Start with what you can do today, even a free cable lock, then upgrade to a safe or lockbox. Talk to other parents before playdates, teach your children what to do if they find a gun, and check your state's storage laws. None of these steps takes long, and together they close the gap that turns curiosity into tragedy.

Frequently asked questions

What is the safest way to store a gun in a home with kids?

Store every firearm unloaded and locked in a safe, lockbox or with a locking device, keep the ammunition locked in a separate place, and keep keys and codes where children cannot reach them. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends this combination for any home with firearms and children.

Is hiding a gun enough if my children are young?

No. Hiding is not storage. Children are curious, they climb and search, and research has found that many children know where a household gun is kept even when their parents believe they do not. A lock is what keeps a child from a firearm.

Should ammunition be stored separately from the gun?

Yes. Pediatric and safety guidance recommends storing ammunition locked and in a different place from the firearm, so a child who gets past one lock still does not have both parts.

Are cable locks safe enough?

A cable lock on an unloaded firearm is a reasonable minimum and much safer than no lock. A safe or lockbox is generally stronger because it protects the whole firearm and is harder to defeat or carry away. Many families start with a free cable lock and upgrade later.

Where can I get a free gun lock?

Many local police departments and sheriff's offices give out free safety kits with a cable lock through Project ChildSafe, a program of the National Shooting Sports Foundation. Call your local agency to ask whether it takes part.

How do I ask other parents if they have guns before a playdate?

Ask simply and without judgment, along with other routine safety questions. For example: we ask everyone this before playdates, are there any guns in the house, and are they locked up? The American Academy of Pediatrics encourages parents to ask.

What should I teach my child to do if they find a gun?

Teach a simple rule and practice it often: stop, do not touch it, leave the area and tell an adult right away. Teaching is a backup to locked storage, not a replacement, because young children may still handle a gun they find.

Are there laws about storing guns around children?

Many states have child access prevention or secure storage laws, and the details differ by state. Federal law requires licensed dealers to provide a safety device with handgun sales. California has its own rules under Penal Code 25100 and SB 53. Check official sources for your state.
